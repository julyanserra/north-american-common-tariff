A proposal for the future of North America

Three economies. Three tariffs. One tariff.

Canada, Mexico and the United States already trade as one continent. They still face the world with three separate tariff schedules. A common external tariff would turn a free trade area into a customs union: one perimeter, one rulebook, one voice.

The thesis

“We propose that North America adopt a common tariff as an anchor for the natural evolution of the free trade area to a customs union.”

— Pedro Noyola & Jaime Serra Puche, A Case for a North American Common Tariff, Wilson Center, 2025

Thirty years after NAFTA, North American production is deeply intertwined: parts cross the borders several times before a finished vehicle, appliance or medical device reaches a buyer. Yet the legal architecture still treats the region as three separate markets that happen to waive tariffs for one another.

The gap between how the region works and how it is governed has a cost: rules-of-origin paperwork, inconsistent treatment of imports from outside the region, and a standing temptation to use tariffs against one’s own partners. A common tariff closes that gap.

02 · The why

A free trade area keeps three borders facing outward.

Under NAFTA and today’s USMCA, each country sets its own tariff on goods from the rest of the world. That single design choice drives much of the friction that follows.

  1. A

    Different outer walls

    If the same good pays a different tariff depending on where it enters the region, importers have every reason to route through the cheapest door.

  2. B

    So every crossing needs a passport

    To stop that, a free trade area needs rules of origin: a good earns duty-free treatment only if enough of it was made in the region. Firms trace every input, calculate regional content and keep the records for years.

  3. C

    And the rules keep tightening

    Instead of modernizing them, the USMCA made the motor-vehicle rule about 20% more restrictive than NAFTA’s (75% regional content, up from 62.5%). Those who benefit will lobby to keep raising the bar, so that rules of origin “threaten to become a protectionist vortex, a permanent source of trade diversion and net social loss.”

  4. D

    Every border crossing has a toll

    A Mexican customs agency at the U.S. border typically costs between 1% and 3% of invoice value. Add duplicate warehouses on both sides, loading, unloading and reloading, shrinkage and waiting time, and all of it is passed on to the final price.

  5. E

    Meanwhile, partners tariff partners

    With no shared external policy, trade policy toward third countries spills back into the region itself. U.S. tariff increases in 2018, and Mexico’s responses, distorted effective protection. The tariffs announced in 2025 risked a retaliatory spiral inside North America.

The 2025 paradox

When the outside pays less than the inside

Under the U.S. tariffs announced on February 1, 2025, the authors point to an absurd result: a Chinese product passing temporarily through Mexico and re-exported to the U.S. as a Chinese good would pay less than a comparable Mexican product that complied with the USMCA rule of origin.

Chinese good, re-exported via Mexico 10%
Mexican good, USMCA-compliant 25%

With a common external tariff, a good pays the same duty wherever it enters North America. There is no back door to police, and no reason to tariff a partner to close one.

03 · Follow a part

One component, two rulebooks.

A component is imported from Asia, assembled into a product in Monterrey and sold in Detroit. Switch between today’s free trade area and a customs union.

  1. Port of entry

    Enters North America

    Pays Mexico’s tariff for that good, which may differ from the U.S. or Canadian rate for the very same item.

    Pays the common North American tariff once, identical at any port in Canada, Mexico or the U.S.

  2. Monterrey

    Assembled into a product

    The manufacturer traces every input’s origin and calculates regional value content to see whether the product will qualify.

    The manufacturer sources from wherever is best. Engineering decisions are made for cost and quality, not for origin math.

  3. Paperwork

    Certification of origin

    An origin certification is issued and supporting records are kept for years, ready for verification or audit.

    Not needed for tariff purposes. The good already paid the common tariff at the perimeter.

  4. Laredo

    Crosses the internal border

    A customs agency charges 1% to 3% of invoice value. Goods are unloaded and reloaded between warehouses on each side. If the product falls short of the origin rule, it pays the U.S. tariff after all.

    Circulates freely as a North American good. The common tariff eliminates origin certification and customs operations for intraregional trade.

  5. Detroit

    Reaches the buyer

    Every cost along the way, from paperwork and agency fees to double handling and waiting time, is passed on to the delivered price.

    Lower transaction costs, one regional market, and supply chains built for the continent rather than for the paperwork.

04 · The proposal

Not a leap. A convergence.

The common tariff is built from what already exists, line by line, within the rules of the World Trade Organization. It would also put into practice the “tariff unification” actions the Mexican government has proposed since 2024.

01

Start where tariffs already match

Begin with a new common tariff vector holding the subheadings whose tariffs are already equal in all three countries. Everything else follows on a transitional schedule.

02

More open “on the whole”

The tariff would use the gaps between applied (MFN) and WTO-bound rates, so the union ends up more open than today’s free trade area, as GATT Article XXIV requires. Mexico’s bound rates sit well above its applied ones, so lowering them can offset selective increases elsewhere.

03

Use the region’s buying power

The tariff would be designed to minimize trade diversion and use the region’s purchasing power. North America imports about $500 billion a year in machinery and equipment and $400 billion in vehicles, 30% to 50% of what its suppliers export. Where demand moves world prices, a calibrated tariff can lower import prices.

04

Unify agriculture, pool quotas

Items with mixed regimes would unify: agricultural imports would face the same ad valorem and/or specific tariff, and quotas would be aggregated and auctioned among importers across the region.

05

Share the revenue fairly

A plausible formula has two parts: a share to the country where goods arrive (the arrival criterion), and the rest distributed by each country’s contribution to intraregional trade (the trade-creation criterion). The EU and the Southern African Customs Union show it can work.

06

Mirror institutions, no supranational bodies

A common policy on antidumping, countervailing duties and safeguards would be run by mirror national systems, anchored in USMCA Articles 316 and 317. Mexico would separate dumping calculations from injury findings by an independent body, as the U.S. and Canada already do.

07

Competition policy instead of internal antidumping

Intraregional antidumping cases would give way to a single competition policy administered by national bodies, with uniform criteria for relevant markets, market power and remedies.

08

Untangle the spaghetti bowl

Each country’s agreements with third countries would be sorted into obligations that are kept (“grandfathering”), limited to those with no impact on border revenue, and those that gradually converge (“phase-outs”).

09

Link Mexico’s exporters to its economy

With one perimeter, the line between temporary and permanent imports stops making sense. Mexico could phase out the special regime for exporters, which today discourages domestic suppliers, alongside improvements in infrastructure, education, security and energy.

05 · One step, clearly defined

The ladder of economic integration.

Trade agreements line up along a continuum, each adding one element of integration to the one below. North America moved from sectoral deals to a free trade area in 1994. The proposal is to take the next step, and only that step.

  1. e.g. ALADI

    Sectoral agreements

    Limited tariff preferences, sector by sector. North America’s own starting point: the 1965 U.S.–Canada Auto Pact.

  2. We are here e.g. North America

    Free trade area

    No tariffs among members; each keeps its own external tariff, so rules of origin are needed.

  3. The proposal e.g. Southern Africa

    Customs union

    Adds a common external tariff and a unified trade policy toward the rest of the world.

  4. e.g. Southern Cone

    Common market

    Adds mobility of factors of production. Not proposed.

  5. e.g. Europe

    Economic union

    Adds a single currency. Not proposed.

A customs union preserves each nation’s sovereignty over immigration, currency, taxation and domestic policy, and the authors design it to work without supranational institutions. What it unifies is the outer tariff and the voice with which the region negotiates with the world.

06 · Imagine

North America, as one.

What a customs union would make possible for families, workers and firms across the continent.

A truck from Monterrey to Montreal with no origin certificate

With a common tariff, goods circulate freely within North America. No origin certification and no customs operations for trade inside the region.

One tariff book

A single schedule for the whole continent, the same at every port from Vancouver to Veracruz.

One voice at the table

A region able to compete with single countries such as China and India and with blocs such as the European Union, using its combined purchasing power.

Scale that lowers costs

One market at continental scale lowers long-term marginal costs across many sectors.

An anchor for what comes next

A customs union could anchor complementary agreements, such as a trilateral circular-mobility agreement to match labor supply and demand, backed by security pacts against fentanyl and human trafficking.

Each country doing what it does best

Mexico could attract intermediate and manufactured goods now made in Southeast Asia; the United States could make capital-intensive industrial goods now sourced from Europe and Northeast Asia; Canada could see higher demand for its ample energy supply.

“A customs union with a common tariff would contribute more effectively than the USMCA to help keep North America as the most productive region of the world.”

— Noyola & Serra, 2025

07 · It has been done

Customs unions are a proven tool.

1910

Southern African Customs Union

The world’s oldest customs union still in operation, with a shared external tariff and pooled revenue.

1968

European customs union

The founding members abolished internal tariffs and adopted a common external tariff. It was the base on which deeper European integration was later built.

1995

Mercosur

Argentina, Brazil, Paraguay and Uruguay adopted a common external tariff, including a regime of exceptions for sensitive goods.

1995

EU–Türkiye customs union

A customs union for industrial goods between economies of very different size and income, without full membership or free movement of people.

08 · The moment

The window is open now.

  1. 1965

    The Auto Pact

    North American integration begins with sector-specific deals such as the U.S.–Canada Auto Pact.

  2. 1994

    NAFTA enters into force

    Negotiated for Mexico by then Secretary of Commerce Jaime Serra Puche. Mexico’s average tariff, about 9% and more than three times the U.S. average, fell below 5%. It was too early for a common tariff without disrupting Mexican production chains, so the region chose a free trade area.

  3. 2020

    USMCA replaces NAFTA

    A chance to fix accumulated distortions went the other way: stricter rules of origin, and a clause forcing the parties to agree to extend in 2026. In the authors’ words, an opportunity to progress to a better model was lost.

  4. 2025

    Tariffs among partners

    On February 1, the U.S. announces tariffs of up to 25% on Mexico and Canada. Noyola and Serra publish “A Case for a North American Common Tariff” with the Wilson Center’s Mexico Institute.

  5. Jul 2026

    The joint review

    The U.S. declines to extend the agreement in its current form while Mexico and Canada support extension. The USMCA remains in force, now subject to annual reviews.

  6. 2027 →

    Choose a destination

    Extending the USMCA with minor tweaks would entrench its distortions. Each annual review is a chance to set a better destination: a customs union.

  7. 2036

    The deadline

    Absent an extension, the USMCA would expire on July 1, 2036.

Implementation, in five stages

Negotiators would become implementers. Instead of give-and-take, a collaborative process to build a common trade policy, one stage at a time, so firms can adapt gradually and without supranational institutions.

I

New tariff vector

Create the common schedule with the subheadings whose tariffs are already equal in all three countries.

II

Non-tariff barriers

Homogenize non-tariff barriers across every subheading of the new vector.

III

Full common tariff

Add the common tariff for all remaining subheadings.

IV

Competition policy

Homogenize national competition policies and institutions.

V

Trade remedies

Homogenize the policies and institutions of contingent protection.

Sequence as proposed by Noyola & Serra (2025)

09 · Hard questions

Fair questions, straight answers.

Does a customs union mean open borders or a shared currency?

No. A customs union covers goods and the tariff applied to them at the regional perimeter. Immigration, currency, taxation and domestic regulation stay national. The authors do suggest that a union could later anchor separate agreements, such as a trilateral circular-mobility program for seasonal labor backed by security cooperation. That is not free movement of people, and there is no common currency.

Wouldn’t this give up national sovereignty to some new bureaucracy?

The design avoids that on purpose. To avoid creating supranational bodies, each country would operate mirror legal systems for trade remedies and competition policy, coordinated through mechanisms the USMCA already contains (Articles 316 and 317). What is pooled is one instrument, the external tariff, in exchange for a stronger collective position and an end to tariff threats among partners.

How does it address concerns about China?

Directly. Transshipment worries arise because goods can enter through the partner with the lower tariff. Under a common external tariff every door charges the same, so the incentive disappears. The region would set one coherent policy toward third countries instead of three, and could use its combined purchasing power where its demand moves world prices.

Is it compatible with WTO rules?

Yes. GATT Article XXIV expressly permits customs unions, provided that on the whole they do not raise barriers against the rest of the world. The main challenge is aligning the common tariff with each country’s WTO-bound rates. Because Mexico’s bound rates sit well above the ones it applies, lowering them can offset selective increases by its partners.

Who would collect the tariff revenue?

The authors propose a two-part formula: a percentage to the country where the goods arrive (the arrival criterion), and the remainder distributed according to each country’s contribution to intraregional trade (the trade-creation criterion). The European Union and the Southern African Customs Union already share revenue under their own formulas.

Why not simply extend the USMCA as it is?

Because it would preserve a free trade area in which exporters must keep certifying that their goods are “substantially” produced in North America, entrenching the distortions inherited from NAFTA and deepened in the USMCA. The review faces two contradictory goals: ever stricter rules of origin, and a more competitive region. A customs union resolves the contradiction.

What would it ask of Mexico?

A lot, and the authors are candid about it. NAFTA was never a panacea. Mexico needs complementary reforms to close the gap between its productive, modern economy and a lagging, informal one: competition, the rule of law and independent institutions. The union would also let Mexico phase out its special regime for exporters, alongside investment in infrastructure, education, security and energy, so that exports draw on domestic suppliers.

Is it politically realistic?

It is ambitious, and governments have been cautious; Mexico’s economy secretary has said a customs union is not in the spirit of the United States. That is why the authors present the idea in broad strokes, to start a conversation, and why the path is staged: begin where tariffs already coincide, then converge step by step. Each annual review of the USMCA is an opportunity to set that destination.
