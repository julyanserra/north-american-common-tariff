A proposal for the future of the continent

Three countries. Three tariffs. One market.

Imagine North America as a customs union: one common tariff at its outer edge, goods moving freely inside, and a single voice in the world. Each nation keeps its own government, currency, laws and borders for people.

01 · The union at a glance

Five changes. Everything else stays national.

A customs union is the step after a free trade area. It changes how goods are treated at the border, and nothing else.

  1. 1

    One common tariff

    Every imported product pays the same duty, whether it lands in Vancouver, Houston or Manzanillo.

  2. 2

    Free circulation

    Once inside North America, goods cross between the three countries with no customs clearance and no certificates of origin.

  3. 3

    Shared trade rules

    One policy on antidumping, safeguards and competition, applied by each country’s own agencies under mirror laws. No new supranational bureaucracy.

  4. 4

    Shared revenue

    Tariff revenue is split by formula: part to the country where goods arrive, the rest according to each country’s contribution to regional trade.

  5. 5

    One voice

    North America negotiates with China, the European Union and India as one market of 521 million people.

Stays national

  • Currency
  • Immigration and citizenship
  • Taxes and budgets
  • Laws and courts
  • Elections and government
  • Defense and foreign policy

Labor mobility is not part of a customs union. The authors suggest addressing it separately, through a managed agreement among neighbors (see “Neighbors already” below).

A

Already the same: lock it in

In 2023, 2,278 tariff lines (40.6%) already carried the same lowest rate in Mexico and the United States, covering 61% of U.S. imports and 66% of Mexico’s. These go first. The region has done this before: under NAFTA, the three countries aligned their outside tariffs on computers and color TV tubes.

40.6% of tariff lines already identical (U.S.–Mexico, 2023)

B

Different: converge to the lowest

Where rates differ, converge on the lowest rate consistent with WTO commitments, as a 2005 trinational task force recommended. The union must be more open on the whole than what it replaces, as GATT Article XXIV requires.

C

Where buying power counts: calibrate

North America imports about $500 billion a year in machinery and equipment and $400 billion in vehicles, 30% to 50% of what its suppliers export. When a buyer is that large, a jointly set tariff can lower the prices the region pays.

D

Farm goods: pool the quotas

Sensitive products such as dairy and sugar would face one duty, and their import quotas would be pooled and auctioned among importers across the whole region.

04 · The demographic advantage

A continent that stays young.

Europe, China and Japan will lose a large share of their workers by 2050. North America’s workforce keeps growing, because its populations complement each other: Mexico is young, the United States and Canada are older.

Mobility, managed and among neighbors

Work where you are needed. Come home.

The authors propose a trilateral circular-mobility agreement: workers move for a season or a project and return home, matching labor supply and demand across the year. To keep out illegal flows of labor from outside the region, it would be backed by security agreements, such as dismantling fentanyl supply networks in northwestern Mexico and fighting human trafficking in southern Mexico.

Europe shows why proximity matters. Over the past decade, immigration has become one of the most polarizing questions in European politics, with debates centered on integration across differences of language, religion and custom.

North America would start from common ground. Research finds that shared language and culture make migration both more likely and smoother for the people involved.

08 · How we get there

Five stages. No leap.

Negotiators become implementers: instead of trading concessions, the three countries build one trade policy step by step, so firms can adapt and no supranational institution is needed.

  1. I

    A new tariff book

    Start with the tariff lines already equal in all three countries.

  2. II

    Same standards

    Align non-tariff barriers on every line in the new book.

  3. III

    Complete the tariff

    Add the common rate for all remaining lines.

  4. IV

    Competition policy

    Harmonize national competition laws and institutions.

  5. V

    Trade remedies

    Harmonize antidumping, countervailing duties and safeguards.

09 · Questions

Straight answers.

Is this a “North American Union” like the European Union?

No. There is no common currency, no free movement of people and no supranational government. It is a customs union: one outer tariff, free circulation of goods, and coordinated trade rules applied by each country’s own institutions.

Who sets the common tariff?

The three governments, jointly, in stages. They start with the thousands of lines where rates already match and converge on the rest over a transition period. Each country keeps its seat and its voice.

Would prices go up?

The design points the other way. The common tariff converges toward the lowest rates and must be more open on the whole than today, as WTO rules require. Removing origin paperwork and border agency fees of 1–3% lowers costs further.

What about China?

Today, goods can enter through whichever partner charges less. With one common tariff every door charges the same, so the back door closes by design, and the region sets one coherent policy toward third countries instead of three.

Who gets the tariff revenue?

A two-part formula: a percentage to the country where goods arrive, and the remainder according to each country’s contribution to regional trade. The EU and the Southern African Customs Union already share revenue under their own formulas.

What about immigration?

Immigration stays national. Any labor mobility would come through a separate, managed circular-mobility agreement among the three neighbors, backed by security cooperation against illegal flows.

What would it ask of Mexico?

Reforms that strengthen the rule of law, competition and independent institutions, and a gradual end to the special regime for exporters so that their suppliers are local. The union is an anchor for that agenda, not a substitute for it.

Is it realistic?

It is ambitious, which is why it is staged. Since July 2026 the USMCA is reviewed every year; each review is a chance to set a destination instead of patching the agreement.
