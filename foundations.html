01 · The argument

From a free trade area to a customs union, in six steps.

Each step rests on evidence further down this page. Follow the chain, or jump to the step you doubt.

  1. 1

    The region already produces as one.

    Some car parts cross the border seven or eight times, and each dollar Mexico exports to the United States carries about 40 cents of U.S. content. Thirty years of free trade cannot be unscrambled.

    EvidenceE1E2On the vision page

  2. 2

    A free trade area polices that integration at every border.

    To cross duty-free, goods must prove they are North American. Customs agencies charge 1–3% of invoice value, and the content rule for cars has climbed from 50% to 75%.

    EvidenceE3On the vision page

  3. 3

    Three outside tariffs drift apart.

    In 2026 the same Chinese electric car pays 102.5% in the United States, 50% in Mexico and 6.1% within Canada’s quota. Rules of origin have to police every gap.

    EvidenceE4On the vision page

  4. 4

    A customs union removes the border inside.

    With one outside tariff, origin no longer matters within the region: no certificates, no customs for goods. Theory and trade law agree it can leave members and outsiders better off.

    EvidenceE5On the vision page

  5. 5

    One tariff, set jointly, puts the region’s buying power to work.

    North America buys 30–50% of what its machinery and vehicle suppliers export. A buyer that large can lower the prices it pays.

    EvidenceE6On the vision page

  6. 6

    It can be built in stages, on a growing workforce.

    National agencies under mirror laws, five stages and no new bureaucracy, in a region whose workforce grows while China’s and Europe’s shrink. Mexico’s own reforms remain the condition.

    EvidenceE7E8E9E10On the vision page

  7. ∴

    Therefore: a common tariff as the anchor of a customs union.

    Read the proposal

02 · The proposal

A common tariff as the anchor for a customs union.

In March 2025, Pedro Noyola and Jaime Serra published “A Case for a North American Common Tariff” with the Wilson Center’s Mexico Institute. They review three decades of integration, explain why the move from NAFTA to the USMCA missed a chance to improve the model, and propose turning the free trade area into a customs union. In their own words:

“We propose that North America adopt a common tariff as an anchor for the natural evolution of the free trade area to a customs union.”

Noyola & Serra, 2025 · Introduction

“The rules of origin threaten to become a protectionist vortex, a permanent source of trade diversion and net social loss.”

Noyola & Serra, 2025 · II. Rules of Origin

“A common tariff would eliminate origin certification and customs operations for intraregional trade.”

Noyola & Serra, 2025 · IV. Towards a Common Tariff

“A customs union with a common tariff would contribute more effectively than the USMCA to help keep North America as the most productive region of the world.”

Noyola & Serra, 2025 · V. Final Remarks

Read the paper at the Wilson Center ↗

Jaime Serra Puche

Economist (PhD, Yale University). As Mexico’s Secretary of Commerce and Industrial Development (1988–1994) he led Mexico’s negotiation of NAFTA, and later served as Secretary of Finance. He is the founder and chairman of SAI Law & Economics.

Pedro Noyola

Economist. He has worked with Jaime Serra on regional trade since the 1990s, including the 1997 report on regional trade agreements cited in the proposal, and is co-author of Modernidad y economía (1999).

03 · A lineage of ideas

Seventy-five years in the making.

A North American customs union is not a new idea. It builds on the founding theory of customs unions and on a series of proposals from all three countries.

  1. 1950
    Jacob VinerTheory

    Introduces the test still used to grade trade agreements: do they create trade, or divert it from more efficient outside suppliers?

  2. 1961
    Bela BalassaTheory

    Describes integration as a ladder: free trade area, customs union, common market, economic union.

  3. 1994
    NAFTATreaty

    The region chooses a free trade area. Mexico still had high and dispersed tariffs, so a common tariff would have disrupted its production chains.

  4. 1997
    Serra, Noyola et al.Report

    Reflections on Regionalism (Carnegie Endowment) sets three rules for agreements that create trade: respect GATT Article XXIV, treat outside investment without discrimination, and keep effective dispute settlement.

  5. 2001
    Robert A. PastorProposal

    Toward a North American Community argues for deepening NAFTA, drawing lessons from Europe’s integration.

  6. 2002
    Wendy DobsonProposal

    The C.D. Howe Institute’s framework for shaping a shared North American economic space.

  7. 2005
    Council on Foreign Relations task forceProposal

    Co-chaired by John Manley, Pedro Aspe and William Weld: a community whose boundaries “would be defined by a common external tariff and an outer security perimeter,” with the tariff negotiated sector by sector at the lowest rate consistent with multilateral obligations.

  8. 2015
    Jaime Serra PucheStudy

    El TLC y la formación de una región (FCE) documents how two decades of NAFTA built a region that produces together, and closes by asking whether the next step is a customs union.

  9. 2025
    Noyola & SerraProposal

    A common tariff as the anchor for a customs union, with a staged plan, a revenue formula and institutions that avoid supranational bodies.

E1

The region already produces together

North American trade is not just exchange between separate economies but production shared across borders.

40¢of U.S. content in each dollar Mexico exports to the United States

On the vision page04 · Built in North America
  • Koopman, Wang & Wei (2014) StudyValue-added accounting shows how much of each export contains inputs from partners: 40 cents of U.S. content per dollar of Mexican exports to the U.S. (2012).
  • Noyola & Serra (2025) ProposalIndustrial production in Mexico and the U.S. became statistically cointegrated after 1994, as did inflation and interest rates.
  • U.S. Census Bureau DataMexico and Canada are the two largest U.S. trading partners (2024).
  • Serra Puche (2015) StudyNAFTA turned Mexico into an export platform: non-oil exports grew from $0.12 billion a day in 1993 to $0.92 billion in 2013. Updated here: $1.76 billion a day in 2025 (INEGI).

E2

Integration that can’t be undone

After a generation of free trade, the three economies produce together so closely that separating them would cost more than integrating further.

7–8×border crossings for some car parts before final assembly

On the vision pageThe scrambled egg theorem
  • Jaime Serra Puche TheoryThe scrambled egg theorem: once the three economies have lived under NAFTA’s free trade, the eggs cannot be unscrambled; the key is to push forward, toward a customs union.
  • Congressional Research Service (2021) PolicySome auto parts cross the border seven or eight times as they are assembled into larger products; some vehicle seats use components from four U.S. states and four Mexican locations.
  • U.S. Census Bureau DataU.S. goods trade with Canada and Mexico grew from $293 billion in 1993 to $1.6 trillion in 2024.
  • Federal Reserve Bank of Dallas (2026) StudyWhen the 2025 tariffs hit, firms certified origin rather than leave: the share of U.S. imports claiming USMCA rose to about 80%.

E3

Rules of origin are costly and protectionist

Proving origin costs firms money, and origin thresholds tend to rise over time, diverting trade.

50% → 75%regional content a car needs to cross duty-free, 1989 to 2023

On the vision page03 · One perimeter
  • Krueger (1993) TheoryFree trade agreements as protectionist devices: rules of origin can extend protection across member markets.
  • Anson, Cadot, Estevadeordal et al. (2005) StudyEstimate the compliance costs of NAFTA’s rules of origin.
  • Carrère & de Melo (2006) StudyShow that different types of NAFTA origin rules carry different costs.
  • Noyola & Serra (2025) ProposalThe USMCA auto rule is about 20% more restrictive than NAFTA’s; a Mexican customs agency at the U.S. border costs 1–3% of invoice value.
  • Federal Reserve Bank of Dallas (2026) StudyWhen the 2025 tariffs exempted USMCA-compliant goods, the share of U.S. imports claiming the agreement rose from about 50% (Mexico) and under 40% (Canada) to about 80%: compliance is possible, and costly.
  • USTR (2026) Policy94.7% of vehicle imports from Canada and Mexico claimed USMCA treatment in 2025; the regional content rule for cars reached 75% in 2023.

E4

Three tariffs drift apart

Without a common tariff, each country sets its own policy toward the same outside supplier, and origin rules must police the gaps.

3different tariffs on the same Chinese electric car in 2026: 102.5%, 50% and 6.1%

On the vision page02 · The common tariff
  • USTR (2024) PolicyThe Section 301 four-year review raised the U.S. tariff on Chinese electric vehicles to 100%.
  • Government of Canada (2024, 2026) PolicyA 100% surtax on Chinese-made electric vehicles from October 2024; in January 2026, a quota of 49,000 vehicles a year at 6.1%.
  • Government of Mexico (2025) PolicyTariffs of up to 50% on vehicles and other goods from countries without a trade agreement with Mexico, in force from 2026.

E5

A customs union beats a free trade area

A customs union removes the need for rules of origin and, if its common tariff is set well, can benefit its members without hurting the rest of the world.

0certificates of origin needed for trade inside a customs union

On the vision page01 · The union at a glance
  • Viner (1950) TheoryTrade creation versus trade diversion: the standard for judging any regional agreement.
  • Kemp & Wan (1976) TheoryA customs union can always choose a common external tariff that leaves no member and no outsider worse off.
  • Krueger (1997) TheoryOn welfare grounds, a customs union is always superior to a free trade area; free trade areas also build more resistance to further liberalization.
  • GATT Article XXIV PolicyPermits customs unions that cover substantially all trade and do not, on the whole, raise barriers to outsiders.

E6

A large buyer can lower the prices it pays

When a region’s demand moves world prices, a calibrated tariff can lower import prices and raise its welfare.

30–50%of their suppliers’ exports: North America’s purchases of machinery and vehicles

On the vision page02 · The common tariff
  • Optimal-tariff theory TheoryDeveloped since Edgeworth in the late 19th century: the welfare-maximizing tariff is positive only for buyers with market power.
  • Broda, Limão & Weinstein (2008) StudyCountries do set higher tariffs on goods where they have more market power.
  • Noyola & Serra (2025) ProposalNorth American imports of machinery and equipment (about $500 billion a year) and vehicles ($400 billion) equal 30–50% of suppliers’ exports.

E7

Institutions without supranationalism

A customs union can run on coordinated national institutions rather than a new bureaucracy.

5stages, each run by existing national agencies under mirror laws

On the vision page08 · How we get there
  • Noyola & Serra (2025) ProposalMirror legal systems for trade remedies and competition policy, anchored in coordination provisions the USMCA already contains; Mexico would separate dumping calculations from injury findings, as Canada (CBSA and CITT) and the U.S. already do.
  • Revenue-sharing precedents PolicyThe EU assigns part of customs revenue to the country of arrival; the Southern African Customs Union shares a common pool by a negotiated formula.
  • CFR (2005) ProposalThe trinational task force proposed a common external tariff without a supranational government or a common currency.

E8

Demography favors North America

Combined, the three populations keep a growing workforce while rival economies shrink.

+5.3%North America’s working-age population, 2025–2050; China −25.3%, EU −17.0%

On the vision page05 · The demographic advantage
  • UN World Population Prospects 2024 DataWorking-age population 2025–2050: North America +5.3%, EU −17.0%, China −25.3%, Japan −25.6% (our calculations).
  • Serra Puche (2015) StudyProjected that North America’s workforce would begin to outgrow China’s around 2014–15. UN data now show China’s working-age population peaked in 2014.
  • Noyola & Serra (2025) ProposalPropose a trilateral circular-mobility agreement to use the region’s combined demographic pyramid.

E9

Shared culture eases mobility

People move more, and integrate more easily, where language and culture are shared.

43Mpeople in the United States speak Spanish at home

On the vision page06 · Neighbors already
  • Adserà & Pytliková (2015) StudyMigration rates rise with linguistic proximity between origin and destination (30 OECD destinations, 1980–2010).
  • Belot & Ederveen (2012) StudyCultural barriers explain migration between developed countries better than traditional economic variables.
  • INEGI 2020 · Pew 2025 · Statistics Canada 2021 · ACS 2023 DataChristian majorities in all three countries; 43 million Spanish speakers in the U.S.; nearly 800,000 U.S.-born residents in Mexico.

The benefits of integration depend on domestic reforms, especially in Mexico.

05 · Open questions

The hard objections, and what the proposal answers.

A customs union asks more of each country than a free trade area does. These are the strongest reasons to doubt it, and the replies.

Will Washington share control of its tariffs?

The objection

Since 2025 the United States has raised tariffs on its own partners, and in the July 2026 joint review it declined to extend the USMCA, which now faces annual reviews. A common tariff means setting outside tariffs together.

The answer

Start where rates are already the same: 40.6% of U.S. and Mexican tariff lines, covering 61% of U.S. imports. Add the rest in stages, so each annual review can lock in a step instead of reopening the agreement.

USMCA joint review (July 2026) · Noyola & Serra (2025)

Does Mexico want it?

The objection

Mexico’s Economy Secretary has said a customs union is “not in the spirit of the United States.”

The answer

The proposal builds on the “tariff unification” actions Mexico’s own government has put forward since 2024, and its first stage covers only lines where the countries already agree.

Opportimes · Noyola & Serra (2025)

Would it divert trade from cheaper suppliers?

The objection

A customs union can push buyers away from efficient outside suppliers toward costlier ones inside the bloc.

The answer

The common rate converges to the lowest current rate, and GATT Article XXIV requires the union, on the whole, to be no more closed than before. A common tariff can be set so that no member and no outsider is worse off.

Viner (1950) · Kemp & Wan (1976) · GATT Art. XXIV

Who keeps the tariff revenue?

The objection

Today each country keeps the duties it collects. One tariff means one pool, and a fight over how to split it.

The answer

A two-part formula: a share for the country where goods arrive, the rest by each country’s contribution to regional trade. The EU and the Southern African Customs Union already share revenue this way.

Noyola & Serra (2025) · EU · SACU

What about dairy, sugar and other sensitive goods?

The objection

Farm trade is where each country protects most, and where import quotas differ most.

The answer

One duty for each product, with the three countries’ import quotas pooled and auctioned among importers across the region.

Noyola & Serra (2025)

Can Mexico’s economy keep up?

The objection

In the decades after NAFTA, Mexico’s total factor productivity fell 0.5% a year, held back by informality, weak competition and legal uncertainty.

The answer

The union is an anchor for reform, not a substitute: it comes with a gradual end to the special export regime, stronger competition policy and independent institutions.

Levy & López-Calva (2023) · Noyola & Serra (2025)

07 · Data & methods

How the numbers were built.

The common tariff table

Rates are most-favored-nation duties for countries without a trade agreement: U.S. Harmonized Tariff Schedule (T-shirts 16.5%, cars 2.5%, light trucks 25%); Canada’s Customs Tariff (vehicles 6.1%, apparel 18%); Mexico’s import-tariff law as reformed on Dec. 29, 2025 (light vehicles 50%, apparel 35%). Computers and phones are duty-free in all three. Averages are WTO simple MFN averages (Canada and U.S. 2025, Mexico 2026). Temporary additional duties imposed since 2025 are excluded. Common rates follow the rules shown and are illustrative.

Demography

UN World Population Prospects 2024, medium variant, single-year ages (wpp2024 data package). North America is the sum of Canada, the U.S. and Mexico; the EU is its 27 members. Working age is 15–64. Median ages are interpolated from single-year distributions.

Trade and energy

U.S. Census Bureau, top trading partners 2024. U.S. Commerce Department, imports by source 2023. U.S. Energy Information Administration: Canada’s share of U.S. crude imports (2023) and pipeline gas exports to Mexico (2024).

People and culture

INEGI 2020 census (religion; U.S.-born residents). Pew Research Center Religious Landscape Study 2023–24 and Mexican-origin population (2021). Statistics Canada 2021 census. American Community Survey 2023 (Spanish spoken at home).

Economic size

Nominal GDP in current U.S. dollars for 2024, rounded, from IMF and national accounts: North America about $33 trillion (roughly 30% of world output), the EU and China about $19 trillion each.

Updating the 2015 charts

Charts from Serra Puche (2015) were rebuilt with the latest data: non-oil exports from INEGI’s merchandise trade balance (2025: $643.6 billion, divided by 365 days); foreign direct investment from the Secretaría de Economía (2025: $40.9 billion, a record); trade openness from the World Bank (exports plus imports, 74% of GDP in 2024); workforce projections from UN WPP 2024. His charts of U.S. content in Mexican exports (2004) and of business-cycle correlation (1981–2013) were not reproduced for lack of comparable recent data.

Trade policy, 2024–2026

Rules of origin: USMCA Chapter 4 and the Congressional Research Service. Use of USMCA preferences: Federal Reserve Bank of Dallas (August 2026) and USTR’s 2026 Autos Report to Congress. Electric-vehicle tariffs: USTR’s Section 301 review (2024), the Government of Canada (2024 surtax, 2026 quota) and Mexico’s 2025 tariff reform. Shipping: Drewry World Container Index, Shanghai–New York, October 22, 2026.

The scrambled egg animation

Illustrative: the three eggs are drawn the same size, and the dots and lines do not stand for quantities. The figures beside it are sourced. Parts crossing borders: CRS IF11387 (2021). U.S. content in Mexican exports: Koopman, Wang & Wei (2014). USMCA claims: Federal Reserve Bank of Dallas (2026).
